Using the = NA() Function

One of my favorite tips is to use the NA () Function in graphs when you do not want a number to appear. Below I have two graphs the first is a graph showing actuals for the 1st 6 months and then forecast for Balance of Year. The actuals are in a solid gray line and the forecast is a dotted blue line.

​

​

Notice how it looks like one line in the graph, but it is actually two lines. Months 7 through 12 of line one have an NA() formula, so they do not appear in the chart vs. using a zero or blank. The forecast line has an NA () in months 1 through 5, and then months 6 through 12 have the applicable numbers. See below what it would look like if I used zero for the graph.

​

​

The use of = NA() can improve the quality of your graphs. In the video below, I share three use cases where I use the =NA () function to improve the quality of my graphs.

Business Partner's Corner

​

Finance folks are often the conscience of the organization. Some examples include:

​

  • We have to keep aggressive spending in check when things are tight.
  • We have to reign in Marketing or Capital spending to ensure we achieve our budgets.
  • We have to make tough decisions like writing off obsolete inventory that may impact bonuses.
  • We have to have challenging conversations with team members who are not delivering.

​

This often involves having tough conversations which is a skill that can be learned. How do we have these conversations in a thoughtful way where we remain valued members of the team?

​

Before we talk about navigating these tough conversations, let’s define a challenging or tough business conversation. It has three elements*:

​

  1. Opinions vary.
  2. The stakes are high.
  3. Emotions run strong.

​

We also tend to avoid these conversations because we are:

​

  • Afraid of a power struggle.
  • Uncomfortable with conflict.
  • Uncertain how the conversation will go.
  • Careful not to disrupt our social standing in the group.

​

There is a way to handle these conversations in a way that maintains psychological safety on the team helps us all become more and more comfortable and competent with these conversations.

​

The foundation is trust and it is important to build trust on the team before engaging in these tough conversations. Trust building happens when you fully engage with team members, listen to them, and have their backs.

​

Here is a four-pronged approach to having a tough conversation:

​

1) Understand – this means not rushing into it and taking time to understand the other person’s perspective and the context. We often look at things only from our perspective. Putting ourselves in their shoes is extremely helpful.

​

2) Prepare – this starts with the end in mind. It includes separating the people from the problem. Preparation also includes anticipating questions and working on win-win solutions. Tough conversations often include you mastering your emotions and physical reactions.

3) Converse – after spending time understanding and preparing it is time to have the conversation. Follow the plan and be direct and empathetic. Folks appreciate candor and not beating around the bush. It is also important to know when to agree to disagree and not prolong a conversation that is going sideways.

​

4) Debrief – last but not least, learn from every experience. What could I have done differently?

​

Little tweaks can make a massive difference. For example, trigger words or phrases like ‘you should’ or ‘but’ can lead to unsuccessful conversations. On the other hand, using ‘I invite you’ or ‘Yes, and’ which is the second city approach can lead to collaborative tough conversations.

​

Good luck with your tough conversations. If you handle them thoughtfully, they can help you grow as a business professional and human being.

​

*Source: Crucial Conversations, by Joseph Grenny, Kerry Patterson, Ron McMillan, Al Switzer, and Emily Gregory.

The FP&A Guy's Tech Tips

​

Should I use Excel?

​

Excel is a jack-of-all-trades. It is one of the most flexible and amazing software programs ever created. People have built nearly everything In Excel (art, databases, games, models, forms, and so much more). The question one should often ask oneself is not whether Excel can do it, but whether Excel is the right tool for the job. Some questions one should consider asking before building that application in Excel.

​

  • How much data am I dealing with? If you are dealing with millions or tens of millions of records Excel might not be the right tool.

​

  • Do multiple users need access to edit the same files at the same time? If this is the case, you might want to consider a tool other than Excel.

​

  • Do I need to scale the solution at an Enterprise level? If you answered yes might want to consider another solution?

​

  • What is the cost of an alternative solution? One of the main reasons we often use Excel is that it is a much lower cost to implement and maintain, and this is something to consider when selecting a solution.

​

  • Does the solution require real-time data integration? If the answer is yes, you will likely want to consider a tool other than Excel.

​

  • Does the team have the technical knowledge to maintain Excel in-house? Need to assess the team's technical skills and custom Excel solutions often require a lot of strong technical knowledge to maintain.

​

The next time you are thinking about building something in Excel, make sure you take time to list the pros and cons and ask yourself should I be building this in Excel, or will it cause me long-term problems?

Ron's Recommendations

​

Leadership and Self Deception, The Arbinger Institute

​

I really enjoyed this book and learned lessons that I can apply in my business and personal life. Thanks to Taylor Otstot for the recommendation!

​

The book is based on a story about an employee (Tom) at a company called Zagrum. Tom was struggling in his personal and business life as the story unfolded. Zagrum was a company that delivered top tier results through their management system of being ‘out of the box.’

​

Tom met with the leaders who developed and nurtured this system (Bud, Kate, and Lou) and learned the lessons of what it means to be ‘in the box’ or ‘out of the box.’

​

The overall message of this book is that we often fall into a trap of sabotaging our success and happiness.

​

Being ‘in the box’ means:

​

  • We often see ourselves as victims.
  • We see ourselves as hard working, important, fair.
  • We inflate our own virtues.
  • We often blame others.

​

Being in the box leads to self-betrayal and creates distances between ourselves and those we work or live with.

​

The goal is to spend more time ‘out of the box’ where:

​

  • We see ourselves and others as people who are part of the team.
  • We appreciate and treat people with respect.
  • We follow our instincts and do things that help ourselves and others.
  • We do not see ourselves as victims or blame others.
  • We take a proactive approach.
  • We can still have tough conversations when necessary.

​

As I reflect on my experiences, I recall a time when I was left out of a conference in sunny and warm Florida (during the coldest part of the winter in Toronto), and I was squarely in the box. I blamed my boss (in my mind). I never spoke to him about it, and I put myself squarely in the victim corner, which got me nowhere.

​

It was a good learning experience and I realized I should have had a conversation with my boss as it was a simple oversight, and he was a kind man with no ill intention.

​

There are some fantastic personal and business examples in this book which has sold over 2 million copies.

​

The story talks about how the Zagrum culture was initially quite toxic and how the leader, Lou Herbert, learned from his mistakes and put 100% of his effort into creating a winning culture (that delivered industry-leading results).

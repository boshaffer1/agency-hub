Stay out of Excel Hell

​

Microsoft recently announced three new formulas that have a lot of people excited. These are only in Beta but will add a lot of versatility to Excel. The new formulas are known as Regex or Regular Expressions and they are sequences of characters that can be used for string search and text parsing. The three formulas are as follows:

​

REGEXTEST: This formula checks if any part of a supplied text matches a regex pattern. For example, you could check if any of your data contains the word “Delta Airlines,” and if it does, it will return True; if not, it returns false.

​

REGEXEXTRACT: Extracts one or more parts of supplied text that match a regex pattern. One example is you could put into your data to match any pattern with letters “[A-z]” and it would return all letters from A-z that are continuous from the data so if my data was 12368-Widget-67894 it would return the word Widget from the example.

​

REGEXREPLACE: Searches for a regex pattern within supplied text and replaces it with different text. In this example you can search for any pattern and replace it with another pattern.

​

The REGEX formulas will becoming to Xlookup in the future which will allow you to search for strings and patterns and return cells that match the patterns extending the capabilities of Xlookup.

​

To learn more check out the Microsoft website explaining the functions and a few YouTube videos on the subject.

Ron's Recommendations

​

Storytelling with data, Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic

A picture (or data visual) can be worth a thousand words, or it can be useless. It can help you advance the conversation as a Finance professional, or it can confuse your audience.

​

People retain 80% of what they see, 20% of what they read and 10% of what they hear. This is a huge benefit of visualizing data as your audience recall goes way up.

​

This brilliant book describes the importance of creating visuals that incite action and talks about the importance of using stories along with visuals.

​

Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic worked at Google and has become one of the world's leaders in data visualization and storytelling.

​

Here are some insights from the book:

​

  • Bad graphs are everywhere. If we become very intentional with our graphs we can stand out within our organizations.

​

  • A great visual without a story is useless. The story is essential. What are you trying to get across to your audience?

​

  • Think like a designer and be intentional when choosing your visual.

​

  • Use color and contrast very intentionally. Too many colors can be distracting. Great use of color helps you direct the focus of the audience very intentionally.

​

  • Ruthlessly declutter your graphs. Remove gridlines and unnecessary information.

​

  • Last but not least, DO NOT USE PIE CHARTS 😊. Paul would love this. Many visualization experts detest pie charts.

​

The book has fantastic examples of these lessons and more.

If you frequently present financial data, it is a must read and can significantly improve your audiences understanding of the data and the actions that the business can move forward with.
